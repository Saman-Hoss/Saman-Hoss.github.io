To predict the number of dengue cases, we developed a minimal-data model with only two parameters that uses only the time series of reported human cases during the target outbreak.

The main idea of this approach comes from the incidence versus cumulative cases curve. This relationship was originally developed for simpler epidemic models, and in this work we showed that it also applies to a much more detailed dengue transmission model that includes humans, mosquitoes, multiple serotypes, and different disease outcomes.

Using this framework, we built a forecasting model that can predict the outbreak trajectory from data at the beginning of the season. We also developed a Bayesian version of the model to account for uncertainty in case reporting and model fitting.

We tested the model on the 2022--2023 dengue outbreaks in Florida. The results showed that the Bayesian model improved predictive performance and reproduced the timing and trajectory of the outbreaks with good accuracy.

A key feature of this model is that it does not require mosquito surveillance, environmental covariates, or long historical records. Because it relies only on reported human cases in the target year, it can be used in data-limited settings and in places where dengue has only recently emerged.

This type of model can support earlier public health action by providing useful lead time for outbreak response. In Florida, the results also suggested a meaningful lag between mosquito abundance and predicted dengue risk, which may help guide the timing of mosquito control efforts.
